Kidney Health

Chronic Kidney Disease

Learn about chronic kidney disease symptoms, stages, causes, diagnosis, treatment, and daily steps to protect kidney health.

Chronic kidney disease, also called CKD, means the kidneys have been damaged or are not working as well as they should for at least three months. The kidneys remove waste and extra fluid from the blood, help control blood pressure, and support healthy red blood cells and bones. CKD often has no symptoms in its early stages. Regular testing and a personalized treatment plan can help protect kidney function, lower the risk of complications, and support overall health.

Common Causes and Risk Factors

Diabetes and high blood pressure are the most common causes of CKD. Other causes and risk factors include:

  • Heart or blood vessel disease
  • Family history of kidney disease
  • Prior kidney injury
  • Repeated kidney infections or kidney stones
  • Autoimmune diseases, such as lupus
  • Polycystic kidney disease and other inherited kidney conditions
  • Long-term use of some medicines that can affect the kidneys
  • Smoking
  • Older age

Possible Symptoms of CKD

Many people feel well during early CKD. As kidney function declines, symptoms may include:

  • Tiredness or weakness
  • Swelling in the feet, ankles, legs, hands, or face
  • Changes in urination, including foamy urine or blood in the urine
  • Shortness of breath
  • Nausea, poor appetite, or changes in taste
  • Itching
  • Muscle cramps
  • Trouble sleeping or concentrating

These symptoms can have many causes. Testing is the best way to understand kidney health.

How Is Chronic Kidney Disease Diagnosed?

CKD is usually identified with blood and urine tests. Common tests include:

  • Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR): A blood-test result that estimates how well the kidneys filter waste.
  • Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR): A urine test that looks for albumin, a protein that can leak into the urine when the kidneys are damaged.
  • Blood pressure measurement: High blood pressure can cause and worsen kidney disease.
  • Imaging or other testing: Ultrasound, CT scan, or other studies may be used to look for structural kidney problems or determine the cause of CKD.

Understanding CKD Stages

CKD is grouped into stages based mainly on eGFR and the amount of albumin in the urine. Lower eGFR levels and higher urine albumin levels may mean a higher risk of CKD progression.

  • Stages 1 and 2: Kidney damage may be present even though kidney filtering is normal or only mildly reduced.
  • Stage 3: Kidney function is moderately reduced.
  • Stage 4: Kidney function is severely reduced.
  • Stage 5: Kidney failure. Dialysis, kidney transplant, or supportive kidney care may be considered based on individual needs and preferences.

Not everyone with CKD progresses to kidney failure. Your care plan and follow-up schedule depend on the cause of CKD, test results, other health conditions, and how kidney function changes over time.

Treatment and Kidney Protection

There is no single treatment for every person with CKD. Treatment focuses on addressing the cause, slowing further kidney damage, managing complications, and reducing the risk of heart disease. Your treatment plan may include:

  • Controlling blood pressure
  • Managing diabetes and blood sugar, when applicable
  • Medicines that help protect the kidneys and heart when appropriate
  • Treatment for anemia, high potassium, bone and mineral changes, or fluid buildup if these develop
  • Reviewing all prescription medicines, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, and supplements for kidney safety
  • Referral to a kidney specialist when needed

Everyday Steps to Support Kidney Health

  • Take medicines exactly as prescribed.
  • Keep scheduled blood and urine testing appointments.
  • Check blood pressure as directed.
  • If you have diabetes, work toward your recommended blood sugar goals.
  • Avoid smoking and secondhand smoke.
  • Stay physically active within your abilities.
  • Choose a balanced eating pattern and limit highly processed foods and excess sodium.
  • Meet with a kidney dietitian if recommended. Protein, potassium, phosphorus, and fluid needs can vary based on your CKD stage, laboratory results, and other health conditions.
  • Avoid starting over-the-counter medicines, herbal products, or supplements without checking whether they are safe for your kidneys.
  • Use nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, such as ibuprofen or naproxen, only if your treatment plan says they are appropriate.

When to Contact Our Office

Contact our office if you have:

  • New or worsening swelling
  • Changes in urine amount, color, or foaminess
  • Increasing fatigue, nausea, itching, or poor appetite
  • Difficulty controlling blood pressure
  • Questions about a new medicine, supplement, or over-the-counter product
  • A recent illness with vomiting, diarrhea, fever, or poor fluid intake

When to Seek Urgent Medical Care

Seek urgent medical care for:

  • Severe shortness of breath or chest pain
  • Fainting, new confusion, or severe weakness
  • Severe swelling that develops quickly
  • Very little or no urine output
  • Persistent vomiting or inability to keep fluids down
  • Blood in the urine with severe pain, fever, or chills

Living Well With CKD

A CKD diagnosis can feel overwhelming, especially when symptoms are not obvious. You do not have to manage it alone. Regular monitoring, healthy daily habits, and a treatment plan tailored to your needs can help protect kidney function and support your quality of life.
